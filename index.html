In short. We tested whether AI Safety via Debate can lead judges to the truth when they start out believing something false. It works for people: debate raised human accuracy by 16 points, while a one-sided expert did not help. In our setting, it does not reliably work for weaker AI models acting as judges, which matters for oversight pipelines where AI supervises AI.

Overview

The scalable oversight problem

Existing approaches to aligning AI systems depend on human feedback: people compare model outputs and indicate which is better, and the model is trained toward those preferences. This works as long as evaluators can tell a good, truthful answer from a bad, false one. As AI systems take on tasks that exceed what their supervisors can check, that feedback becomes unreliable, and errors, deception or harmful behavior can go unnoticed. Scalable oversight is the research problem of keeping supervision meaningful as AI capabilities outgrow those of the people, or the systems, that supervise them.

AI Safety via Debate

Debate, proposed by Irving, Christiano and Amodei in 2018, is one potential answer to that problem. Instead of evaluating a single answer, a judge reads an exchange between two equally capable AI systems that argue for opposite answers to the same question. The judge, a person or a weaker AI model, then decides which side is right, and that verdict becomes the training signal. The setup resembles a trial, where a jury that did not witness the events must decide, from two adversarial accounts, what really happened.

“In the debate game, it is harder to lie than to refute a lie.”The central assumption of debate (Irving et al., 2018)

If that holds, the honest debater has the advantage, and training on judges' verdicts rewards truthful behavior even when the judge lacks the expertise to verify the answer directly.

Why prior beliefs matter

Judges are not blank slates. People bring prior beliefs, intuitions and biases to every decision, and a capable system can learn to exploit them, a failure mode known as judge hacking. For debate to work as an effective oversight tool, it should move judges toward the truth even when they start out wrong. The same applies to AI judges, since many proposed oversight pipelines rely on weaker models supervising stronger ones.

What we did

We built a dataset of claims about ten real scientific and technological developments that most people know little about, such as mirror life, nuclear fusion and black hole imaging. We recorded each judge's prior belief, so that about half started from the correct general view and half from an incorrect one, and then measured whether reading a debate changed their answer. We compared debate with consultancy, where a single AI argues one side; checked whether corrections carry over to related claims; and tested groups of judges that vote or deliberate, including mixed panels of people and AI models.

    Key findings

    Cross-subtopic debate: does a correction carry over?

    Debate studies usually ask judges about the same claim that was just debated. We also asked about a different claim on the same topic, to see whether debate changes the judge's underlying belief about the general topic and not only their answer to one question. This allows us to assess whether belief updates induced by the debate generalize beyond the specific statement discussed, thereby testing the robustness of debate in terms of belief revision across related subtopics.
