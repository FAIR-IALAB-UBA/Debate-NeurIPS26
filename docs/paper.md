4.2. Results 
Our primary outcomes are post-belief correctness and changes in self-reported confidence, with pre-belief type (mainstream vs. skeptical) used as a key moderator. Because all tasks share a common ground truth (Statement 1 is correct), we can compare accuracy consistently across protocols. Analytically, we apply paired tests (McNemar and Wilcoxon) for within-item pre–post comparisons in in-subtopic and consultancy settings, while cross-subtopic conditions are analyzed using between-item comparisons with binomial baselines and cluster-robust uncertainty estimates due to non-aligned pre/post items. Cross-protocol differences are evaluated using Fisher’s exact tests for binary correctness and Mann–Whitney tests on per-subject accuracy changes. All families of statistical tests are corrected for multiple comparisons using Holm–Bonferroni adjustments within and across protocols.
Highlight 1: Debate outperforms consultancy and improves accuracy, particularly among erroneous judges. Debate (n = 333) significantly increased post-debate accuracy (from 50.5% to 61.3%, p = 0.0001), whereas consultancy (N = 80) showed no improvement (50.6% to 48.7%). The effect is particularly pronounced for the erroneous group (n = 45), which exhibits a +25 percentage point gain (McNemar raw p = 0.007). Moreover, consultancy significantly increased judges’ confidence (from 58 to 73 mean confidence), reinforcing their prior beliefs. 
Highlight 2: In-subtopic and cross-subtopic debate yield nearly identical post-accuracy, with 57.6% (n = 92) and 58.4% (n = 89), respectively (Δ = −0.8 pp). This difference is not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact p ≈ 1.0), and the bootstrap confidence interval on the difference is wide and centered around zero (−15.0 to +13.3 pp). The same null result holds within general pre-belief strata. Overall, these results indicate no detectable difference between conditions, suggesting that the debate effect transfers fully across related subtopics.
Highlight 3: Multi judge. Hybrid multi-judge deliberation modestly improves accuracy from 50.5% to 57.1% (+6.7 pp, n = 105), but this effect does not reach statistical significance (McNemar p = 0.296; binomial p = 0.172 two-sided, 0.087 one-sided). In contrast, confidence increases substantially from 58 to 73 (Wilcoxon p < 0.001). 
Highlight 4: Highly-confident post-debate judgments are more reliable. We evaluate whether post-debate confidence can serve as a signal for selective prediction, i.e., whether filtering judgements by confidence improves accuracy among retained cases. We apply a rejection setup in which, for a threshold τ, we retain only judgements with post-confidence ≥ τ and report coverage and selective accuracy. Across debate (n = 333), selective accuracy increases as τ rises, from 61.3% with no filtering to 74.0% at τ = 75 and 75.0% at τ = 85, with decreasing coverage (27.6%). The same trend holds in the in-subtopic subset (n = 92), reaching 72% accuracy at τ = 85 versus a 58% baseline. Overall, these results suggest that post-debate confidence is a meaningful predictor of correctness.
Highlight 5: Judges with lower pre-debate confidence benefit more from debate. We partition the pooled debate sample (n = 333) into low and high pre-confidence strata using multiple thresholds (median, 50, and 70) and compute the change in accuracy (Δ accuracy) within each group. Across all splits, low-pre-confidence judges consistently show larger improvements, with gains exceeding those of high-pre-confidence judges by approximately +3.8 to +4.6 percentage points in the pooled sample. A similar pattern is stronger in the in-subtopic subset (n = 92), where low-pre-confidence judges gain +11 to +13 percentage points more than their high-pre-confidence counterparts. However, Mann–Whitney tests on per-subject Δ scores do not reach statistical significance in any specification (all p > 0.15), suggesting that while the direction of the effect is stable across splits and datasets, it is not statistically conclusive at this sample size.
